Welcome to HowTall?

by Rutherford ecology

How accurate are these heights?

HowTall? reads heights straight from LINZ's LiDAR surveys. For most trees they're within about half a metre of a measured height, and often closer. They can be further out:

  • Steep slopes: height is measured from the ground directly under the treetop. A tree leaning downhill can read a metre or more taller than measured from its base.
  • Bluffs and cliff edges: these can show up as trees that aren't there.
  • Narrow, pointed crowns: the laser can miss the very tip, so young conifers and cabbage trees may read a little short.
  • Old surveys: heights are for the date the survey was flown. Trees grow, and some are felled since. The year is shown under each result.

Where an exact height matters, check it on the ground.

HowTall?

Tap a tree and HowTall? tells you how tall it is, from the LiDAR survey of that spot, published by Toitū Te Whenua LINZ. New Zealand only, wherever LINZ has a 1 m surface model.

What a tap gives you

A tap reads a circle round the spot; the slider sets its radius, 10 to 200 m (up to about 12.5 ha). You get the tallest tree in the circle, the tree you tapped (the highest point within 3 m of the tap, since a crown's top is rarely exactly where a finger lands), the five tallest tree tops, and how much of the circle is over 2 m. The tallest tree is marked pink on the map, the other tall tops green. Other trees get a small dot only if they stand out: one that rises at least 3 m (or a tenth of its height) above the canopy round it, like an emergent over the forest or a lone tree in a paddock. Trees in an even canopy don't get a dot. Tap a dot to see its height. Two tops count as separate trees only if the canopy dips between them by at least 2 m, or a quarter of the shorter tree's height, so the bumps on one broad crown get one dot.

The target button on the map uses your phone's location; Location (GPS) needs to be on in the phone's settings. Under a forest canopy GPS is often out by 5 to 20 m, so the circle is widened to at least your GPS accuracy.

Aerial, Ground and Topo

Aerial shows the photo with tree heights coloured over it. Ground takes away the photo and the trees: inside the circle it shows the bare earth from the LiDAR's ground model at 1 m, shaded as if lit from the north-west, so tracks, streams, slips and old earthworks under the canopy show up. Outside the circle is Esri's much coarser world hillshade, for context. Topo is LINZ's NZ Topo50 map (contours, tracks, huts) with the tree heights coloured over it; zoomed well out it's OpenTopoMap.

How heights are worked out

Height is the surface model (the top of everything) minus the ground model (the bare earth under it), both from the same flight, on a 1 m grid. Things that are clearly power lines or pylons are left out: a cell far above everything round it and too narrow to be a crown. Buildings are not left out, so a tap on a roof gives the roof.

The 1 m grid can clip the very tip of a narrow, pointed crown (a young conifer, a cabbage tree), so the true top may be a metre or two higher. Heights are for the date the survey was flown; a tree may have grown since. When more than one survey covers the spot, the newest is used; the line under the result says when it was flown and whose it is.

Data used

Nothing is downloaded until you tap. Then HowTall? reads just the 1 m cells around the spot from LINZ's Cloud-Optimised GeoTIFFs, in blocks of 512 × 512 m: about 0.75 MB a tap for a small circle, about 2.5 MB for a 200 m circle, more where it straddles block edges. Blocks are kept, so tapping again nearby downloads nothing.
