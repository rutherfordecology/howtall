Welcome to HowTall?

by Rutherford ecology

How accurate are these heights?

HowTall? reads heights straight from LINZ's LiDAR surveys. For most trees they're within about half a metre of a measured height, and often closer. They can be further out:

  • Steep slopes: height is measured from the ground directly under the treetop. A tree leaning downhill can read a metre or more taller than measured from its base.
  • Bluffs and cliff edges: these can show up as trees that aren't there.
  • Narrow, pointed crowns: the laser can miss the very tip, so young conifers and cabbage trees may read a little short.
  • Old surveys: heights are for the date the survey was flown. Trees grow, and some are felled since. The year is shown under each result.

Where an exact height matters, check it on the ground.

HowTall? web

The computer version of HowTall?: tree heights over a whole block, from the LiDAR survey of that ground, published by Toitū Te Whenua LINZ. New Zealand only, wherever LINZ has a 1 m surface model.

Circle or drawn area

Circle: click the map and HowTall? reads a circle round the spot, 10 to 500 m radius (up to about 78 ha). Draw an area: click round a block to put down its corners, up to 100 ha, and finish with a double-click or by clicking the first corner.

You get the tallest tree, the number of separate trees over 5 m, the ten tallest (click one to go to it), how much of the area is in each height class, and its mean canopy height. The tallest tree is marked pink, the other tall tops green, and trees that stand out get a small dot: one that rises at least 3 m (or a tenth of its height) above the canopy round it, like an emergent over the forest or a lone tree in a paddock. Click a dot to see its height. Two tops count as separate trees only if the canopy dips between them by at least 2 m, or a quarter of the shorter tree's height, so the bumps on one broad crown count once.

Tall trees pink, under the result, colours the tallest of the canopy pink and the rest in muted greens, so the tall trees jump out (as inferrd's Emergents). Pink starts at the tallest 10% of the cover over 2 m; the slider sets your own height, which then holds for every area until Auto.

Marking trees: click a tree's dot, or right-click any tree in the area read, and give it a name. Marked trees stay on the map with their names, are listed in the panel (click one to go to it, rename it or unmark it), and their names go into the CSVs. Marked trees (CSV) downloads just them. Marks are kept in this browser only, so they won't show on another computer or if the browser's site data is cleared: download them to keep a copy.

All trees (CSV) gives every tree over 5 m in the area; Tall trees only (CSV) only those at or over the pink height. Each row has the tree's height, how far it rises above the canopy round it, the ground under it, and where it is: NZTM2000 easting and northing (EPSG:2193, the middle of the 1 m cell holding its top) and latitude/longitude. The tree count is from the LiDAR's crown tops, so trees under a taller canopy are missed and a big spreading crown can count as two.

Where an area is covered by more than one survey, the newest is used, and the gaps it leaves are filled from older ones; the line under the result names each survey used.

Aerial, Ground and Topo

Aerial shows the photo with tree heights coloured over it. Ground takes away the photo and the trees: inside the circle it shows the bare earth from the LiDAR's ground model at 1 m, shaded as if lit from the north-west, so tracks, streams, slips and old earthworks under the canopy show up. Outside the circle is Esri's much coarser world hillshade, for context. Topo is LINZ's NZ Topo50 map (contours, tracks, huts) with the tree heights coloured over it; zoomed well out it's OpenTopoMap.

How heights are worked out

Height is the surface model (the top of everything) minus the ground model (the bare earth under it), both from the same flight, on a 1 m grid. Things that are clearly power lines or pylons are left out: a cell far above everything round it and too narrow to be a crown. Buildings are not left out, so a tap on a roof gives the roof.

The 1 m grid can clip the very tip of a narrow, pointed crown (a young conifer, a cabbage tree), so the true top may be a metre or two higher. Heights are for the date the survey was flown; a tree may have grown since. When more than one survey covers the spot, the newest is used; the line under the result says when it was flown and whose it is.

Data used

Nothing is downloaded until you click or finish an area. Then HowTall? reads the 1 m cells under it from LINZ's Cloud-Optimised GeoTIFFs, in blocks of 512 × 512 m: about 2.5 MB for a 200 m circle, roughly 5 to 15 MB for 100 ha, more where it straddles block edges or needs an older survey to fill gaps. Blocks are kept while the page is open, so reading again nearby downloads nothing.
